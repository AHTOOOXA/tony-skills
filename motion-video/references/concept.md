# Concept: finding the idea

The craft is solved by the tools; the idea is where films win or die. Use as much of this as the job needs —
for a 6-second sting, one line may do; for a launch or a rebrand, do the whole thing. Write the result to a
short `CONCEPT.md` before building.

## 1. Find the story

- **What kind of film, at what level?** A product demo can be one execution; a rebrand or a launch needs a
  claim about the *brand* — who it is now and why — not a tour of its assets.
- **The message as a claim, not a topic.** "We stopped shouting" / "the key is to the city, not the flat" —
  not "our new identity". If you can't write it as a sentence someone could disagree with, you don't have it.
- **The tension.** One sentence: *[the viewer] wants ___, but ___ stands in the way, because ___* — or for a
  character film, *X wants ___, but ___, so ___*. For a rebrand the "but" is the reason for the change.
- **Material from the source.** The product's real UI, its own words (tagline, manifesto, the verbs it uses),
  its objects, its facts. Props come from here: if a prop could appear unchanged in another brand's film, it
  didn't come from the source.
- **Write the typical film in one line** (§6) — that's what everyone else would make. Your idea should beat it,
  invert it, or at least know it's choosing it.

## 2. Generate ideas

Go wide before going deep. The first 2–3 ideas are usually the typical film — treat them as warm-up. Aim for
5 that differ in their **central picture** (not in palette or copy), and make sure at least one or two are
ideas a model would rarely produce. Two ideas that would look the same on a contact sheet are one idea.

Generators that work (combine freely):
- **Name the enemy / negate the category.** What does the brand refuse? Make the refusal act one.
  (Airbnb "Don't go there. Live there.": tourism is the enemy.) Often the brand's own slogan already contains
  the enemy — read it for double meanings.
- **Make the feeling literal.** Stage the benefit as a physical event (HomePod "Welcome Home": music makes a
  cramped flat expand).
- **The transition is the argument.** Find one transformation that *is* the message and build the film around
  that single move (the old logo's "O" becomes a keyhole and the camera flies through it into the world).
- **One element becomes everything.** A dot becomes the caret, the list, the loader; a hero object is the only
  saturated colour in the film and carries every scene.
- **What changed and why — one reason per change.** Old → new → the viewer-facing reason → the picture that
  shows the reason (Slack: the old mark "broke" off-angle, so show it breaking; Wise: "our name catches up
  with who we're already building for").
- **The identity living in the world.** Give each element a job in a real moment — the colour is a time of
  day, the type appears only where someone reads it, the mark is on the thing in their hand. Never on an
  empty background.
- **Customer as hero, brand as guide.** The viewer's character has a problem and the product is the tool in
  their pocket.
- **Hero object + a coined word.** One object holds the promise; the film teaches one phrase.
- **Old way as archive, new way as present.** The past is grainy footage on an old screen; the cut to the
  present (on the drop) is the reveal.
- **Borrow a genre.** A trailer, a departure board, a nature documentary, a museum exhibit, an
  over-produced mobile-game ad, a receipt, an error log, a weather report. The genre brings its own structure.
- **Launch it as something absurdly bigger** (a baguette launched like an iPhone).
- **Structural templates on the brand's own elements** (SIT): subtraction (remove the old mark from the places
  it failed), multiplication, division, task unification (one element does two jobs), attribute dependency
  (the colour depends on the time / the user / the music), pictorial analogy (the mark as an object that does
  something).
- **Metaphor mapping.** List the properties of the benefit; list 8+ candidate objects; pick the one with the
  most overlaps.
- **Shapes of motion** (onetake): one camera move through scale; a before/after split until one side breaks
  into the other; a chain reaction where each step produces the next.

Each idea in five lines: the idea as a **picture** · the **spine** (what carries across every cut) · the
**hook frame** (0–2 s: the action + any words) · what it **rules out** · the **look** (a named reference).

## 3. Pick one

Quick gates: can you say it in one sentence? Does the message land by beat 2? Is there a **visual engine**
(something to watch, not text explaining)? Is it built from real assets/facts? Can you build it in code in the
time you have?
Then judge originality, fit, emotion, simplicity; for your favourite write "why isn't this a 9?" and fix that
once. If the user is around and it's their call, show all five *before* your recommendation (a recommendation
first anchors everything after it); mixing two is a fine answer. Running alone: commit, and note which typical
film you left behind.

## 4. Shape it

- One line on top: *This film tells [audience] that [claim].*
- The **spine** named before the beats: one element that transforms, a hero prop with a callback, or a chain.
- Beats: each has an **event** (something happens), a **why** that traces to the claim, and something that
  **carries** into the next. A beat whose why doesn't trace to the claim is a beat to cut.
- One **breather** (a held moment); a **callback** (the opening returns, fuller); the ending rhymes with the opening.
- Brand timing fits the goal: a brand film can reveal the name last; a performance ad shows it in the first seconds.
- Before full animation: stills of every beat, or one 6–8 s section built for real, then the rest.

## 5. Quick tests that catch a tour

- **The deletion test.** Delete the evidence beats — does the claim still stand? Delete the claim beats — if the
  film still "works", it was a feature tour.
- **The chapter test.** Does the beat list read like a brandbook's table of contents (logo → colour → type →
  application) or a website's sections (hero → 3 features → stat → CTA)? Then it's inventory.
- **The empty-background test.** Is any identity element shown alone on a flat field with a label? It has no job.
- **The swap test.** Could you put another brand's name on it? Push the idea further.
- **The silent one-sentence test.** Muted, can a fresh viewer (a sub-agent looking at stills) say what it's about
  in one sentence?

## 6. The typical film, per kind (what to beat)

| Kind | The typical film | Usually stronger |
|---|---|---|
| rebrand | logo morph → palette swatches → type specimen → end card | the reason for the change as a picture; the identity living in moments |
| app showcase | hero screen → 3 feature cards → stat → CTA | one user's moment from problem to relief, the real UI doing the job |
| launch / teaser | logo sting → "introducing" → feature montage → URL | one surprising capability shown happening, or the product launched as something bigger |
| explainer | problem text → diagram → solution text | the mechanism as a physical metaphor the viewer can watch |
| logo animation | lines draw on → fill → glow | the mark *does* what the brand does |

## 7. Films that argued one idea

| Film | Device |
|---|---|
| Apple "1984" | an enemy (conformity) smashed; the product never shown |
| Apple HomePod "Welcome Home" | the feeling made physical: a flat expands with the music |
| Airbnb Bélo (2014) | the symbol introduced as a promise ("wherever you see it, you belong"), not as artwork |
| Airbnb "Don't go there. Live there." (2016) | category negation: tourism is the enemy, living like a local the vision |
| Google logo (2015) | the change performed on the brand's own canvas: a hand wipes the old logo and draws the new |
| Slack (2019) | a confession: the old mark failed off its angle; show the failure, then the fix |
| Instagram (2016) | memory → geometry: "the rainbow lives on" |
| Spotify (2015) | the identity is how music feels (duotone on real artists), not a logo slide |
| Duolingo (2019) | one character generates the whole system |
| Uber (2018) | the hero shifts from the company to the people moving |
